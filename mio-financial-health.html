Case Study · MiO Investor App · VinaCapital · Summer 2026

Turning a financial-health check-in into a signal the business could actually use.

RoleBusiness Solutions Intern (Research)
ProductMiO, VinaCapital's consumer investor app
RelatedFeature-design case study →

This is the second half of the Financial Health work I did at VinaCapital. The design case study covers why we built a lightweight financial-health check-in for MiO's investors and how we scoped it down to three questions. This one is the question the fund business asked once that feature existed: now that investors are telling us something real about their financial situation, can we use it to sell them the right fund — without it feeling like a bait-and-switch?

Problem

MiO's retail investors and VinaCapital's actual fund business were, organizationally, two different conversations. Investors used the app to buy and track a fund they'd usually chosen on their own or by referral. Meanwhile the fund team had a real business problem: a lot of investors sat in a single fund that no longer matched their situation, and reaching out to suggest a better fit read as sales outreach, so almost no one did it.

The Financial Health check-in had, by accident, created something the fund team didn't have before: a small amount of investor-reported context — risk comfort, time horizon signals, whether they had a cushion set aside — collected at a moment investors had already opted into being asked about their finances. The open question was whether that context was usable for anything beyond the retention purpose it was built for.

Research question

Under what conditions does a financial-health signal create a legitimate opening to point an investor toward a specific fund, rather than feel manipulative, generic, or like a compliance problem waiting to happen?

Approach

  • Mapped existing fund choice against financial-health responses. For investors who'd already completed the check-in, I looked at whether their answers (risk comfort, horizon, emergency-fund status) lined up with the fund category they'd actually chosen — money market, balanced, or growth equity — to see if there was a legible pattern, or if fund choice was mostly driven by referral and habit rather than fit. The framing that made the idea workable: the recommendation couldn't be "buy this fund." It had to be observational and optional — closer to "investors with a similar profile to yours often look at [category]" than a push notification with a sell button. That kept it inside the same compliance lane as the original check-in, and it meant the feature stayed useful to the investor first, which was the only way the fund business's interest in it wouldn't undercut the trust the original feature was built to earn.

Trade-offs & constraints

  • Usefulness to the investor vs. usefulness to the business. The version of this that would have moved the most fund AUM was also the version that would have made the Financial Health feature feel like a trap. I optimized for the version investors would still trust in six months, which meant leaving real revenue upside on the table in the short term.
  • Signal vs. sample size. The financial-health data was still thin — the check-in had only existed for [TODO: how long]. Any pattern I found needed a "revisit once we have N more responses" caveat, which is an honest but less satisfying way to end a recommendation memo.
  • Compliance review adds a step the fund team doesn't love. Every version of the recommendation copy had to clear the same "would this survive an audit" test as the original feature, which slowed down how quickly this could ship relative to how the fund team wanted to move.

What I took away

Every company I've worked with eventually asks some version of "we have this data, can we monetize it?" The PM's actual job in that moment isn't to say yes or no — it's to figure out whether the thing you're proposing still respects why the user gave you the data in the first place. The Financial Health feature only worked because investors believed it existed for them. The moment a recommendation built on top of it stopped being true to that, the whole feature's credibility was at risk, not just the new recommendation.

This is also the project that taught me the difference between a feature case study and a research case study. One is "here's what I built." The other is "here's the question I chased and what I'd tell you even though the answer isn't fully proven yet." Recruiters mostly hear the first kind. I think the second kind is the more honest signal of how someone actually thinks.
